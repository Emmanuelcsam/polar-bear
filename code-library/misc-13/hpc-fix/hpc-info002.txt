A Comprehensive Guide to High-Performance Computing on the William & Mary Bora Subcluster




Section 1: An Introduction to the W&M HPC Ecosystem




1.1. Overview of Research Computing at William & Mary


The William & Mary (W&M) Research Computing group provides a robust infrastructure of supercomputers and computer clusters designed to address advanced computational problems. These problems are typically characterized as being highly computation-intensive, data-intensive, or both, rendering them intractable on standard workstations or laptops.1 The High-Performance Computing (HPC) resources at W&M facilitate cutting-edge research across a diverse and expanding array of disciplines, including but not limited to ocean modeling, quantum and particle physics, health sciences, mathematics, operations research, Bayesian statistics, and econometrics.
These powerful systems enable researchers to simulate complex phenomena and analyze massive datasets, providing insights that traditional laboratory experiments cannot accommodate. The combined computational capacity of the HPC resources across the main campus and the Virginia Institute of Marine Science (VIMS) campus is approximately 600 trillion double-precision floating-point operations per second (600 TFLOPS). These resources are available at no cost to all W&M faculty, staff, and students for academic research and classroom projects, representing a significant institutional investment in the research mission of the university. The primary HPC environments are the SciClone cluster, located on the main campus, and the Chesapeake cluster at VIMS.


1.2. The SciClone Cluster and the Role of the Bora Subcluster


The main-campus HPC resource, known collectively as SciClone, is not a single, monolithic supercomputer. Instead, it is a heterogeneous environment composed of multiple, distinct subclusters, each with specific hardware and a designated purpose.3 This architectural design allows for the optimization of resources based on the nature of the computational task. The subclusters are generally categorized into two primary types: those designed for multi-node parallel calculations (often utilizing the Message Passing Interface, or MPI) and those better suited for serial (single-core) or shared-memory (multi-core, single-node) applications, including jobs that require Graphics Processing Units (GPUs).3
The Bora subcluster is a key component of the SciClone environment and is explicitly intended for multi-node parallel jobs.4 It is equipped with Intel Xeon "Broadwell" processors and is architected to handle large-scale computations that span multiple physical machines working in concert.3 This specialization means that Bora is the appropriate and most efficient choice for researchers running MPI-based applications that require the coordinated power of tens or hundreds of processor cores. Conversely, it is an inefficient and inappropriate choice for serial or small-scale shared-memory jobs, which should be directed to other subclusters, such as Hima.4 Understanding this fundamental principle of matching the computational workload to the correct hardware is the first step toward effective and efficient use of the W&M HPC resources.


1.3. Core Concepts: Nodes, Cores, Schedulers, and Parallelism


To effectively utilize an HPC environment like SciClone, it is essential to understand its basic components and terminology. An HPC cluster is a collection of individual computers, called nodes, that are networked together to function as a single, powerful system.2 Each node contains one or more processors, which in turn contain multiple processing
cores.6 It is the large number of available cores that provides the massive computational power of the cluster.
Users do not log in directly to these compute nodes to run their calculations. Instead, they interact with one or more front-end (or login) nodes.7 From the front-end node, users prepare their calculations, manage files, and submit their computational tasks to a
batch system, also known as a scheduler or resource manager.7 At W&M, the scheduler is the
Simple Linux Utility for Resource Management (Slurm).9 The scheduler's role is to manage the queue of submitted jobs, allocate the requested compute nodes and cores to those jobs when they become available, and ensure that resources are distributed fairly and efficiently among all users.10 This managed environment prevents individual users from interfering with one another and maximizes the utilization of the entire cluster.5 All interaction with the cluster is performed via a command-line interface (CLI), which requires proficiency with a Unix/Linux shell environment.2


1.4. Essential First Steps: Account Activation and Getting Help


Access to the W&M HPC clusters is not granted by default with a standard university account. It is a specialized service that must be explicitly requested.7 Any faculty, staff, or student needing to use the HPC resources for a research or academic project must first submit an account request through the Information Technology website.1 Accounts are granted for a specific duration and will expire, at which point files are subject to deletion. It is the user's responsibility to renew the account or arrange for data transfer before expiration.7
For all questions, software installation requests, debugging assistance, and general support, the central point of contact is the HPC help desk email: hpc-help@wm.edu.7 The Research Computing staff provides support during regular business hours.12 When reporting a problem, it is critical to provide as much relevant information as possible to facilitate a prompt and accurate resolution. A proper help request should include 12:
* The date and time the problem occurred.
* The specific server or compute node(s) involved.
* The exact text of the command(s) that were issued.
* The complete and exact text of any error messages produced.
* Relevant source code, compilation commands (Makefiles), and job submission scripts.
* Any other information that may help diagnose the issue.
Providing this level of detail demonstrates an understanding of the shared research environment and enables the support staff to address complex issues efficiently. For general IT issues unrelated to HPC (e.g., email, Banner), users should contact the main Technology Support Center (TSC) at support@wm.edu or 757-221-4357.12


Section 2: Connecting to and Navigating the Bora Environment




2.1. The Secure Shell (SSH) Protocol: Your Gateway to the Cluster


All interaction with the W&M HPC clusters is conducted through the Secure Shell (SSH) protocol. SSH provides an encrypted, secure channel for logging into and executing commands on a remote computer.5 The designated front-end node for the Bora subcluster, which also serves the Hima subcluster, is
bora.sciclone.wm.edu.4 This hostname is the single point of entry for compiling code, managing files, and submitting jobs to the Bora compute nodes.


2.2. Connection Procedures: On-Campus, Off-Campus (VPN/Bastion), and X11 Forwarding


Access to the HPC systems is strictly controlled and is limited to connections originating from within the campus network. The method for connecting via SSH depends on the user's physical location.7
* On-Campus Connection: When connected to the W&M network (either on the main campus or VIMS), a direct SSH connection can be established. The command, executed from a local terminal, is 15:
Bash
ssh <username>@bora.sciclone.wm.edu

Where <username> is your W&M userid. If your local machine username is the same as your W&M userid, you can omit the <username>@ portion.
* Off-Campus Connection: To connect from an off-campus location, the connection must first be routed through the university's network. There are two primary methods to achieve this 7:
   1. W&M VPN: The recommended method is to connect to the W&M Virtual Private Network (VPN) first. Once the VPN connection is active, your computer is effectively on the campus network, and you can use the direct on-campus ssh command shown above.
   2. Bastion Host: If not using the VPN, you must "hop" through the IT bastion host. This is accomplished using the -J (JumpHost) flag in the ssh command. This single command securely tunnels your connection through bastion.wm.edu to the final destination.15
Bash
ssh -J <username>@bastion.wm.edu <username>@bora.sciclone.wm.edu

This process will typically require two password entries and Duo two-factor authentication for the bastion host, followed by another password entry for the Bora front-end.15 Failure to use one of these methods from off-campus will result in connection errors such as
Connection timed out or Network is unreachable.7
      * X11 Forwarding: If you need to run graphical applications from the cluster and have them display on your local machine, you must enable X11 forwarding. This is done by adding the -Y flag to your ssh command. For example, an off-campus connection with X11 forwarding would be 15:
Bash
ssh -Y -J <username>@bastion.wm.edu <username>@bora.sciclone.wm.edu



2.3. The tcsh Shell: Understanding W&M's Default Environment


A significant and critical aspect of the W&M HPC environment is that the default command-line shell for all users is the TENEX C shell (tcsh).14 This is a departure from the Bourne-Again Shell (
bash) that is the default on most modern Linux distributions and is featured in the majority of online tutorials.2 While a
bash environment can be requested by emailing hpc-help@wm.edu, all default configurations, system-wide startup scripts, and example job scripts are designed for tcsh.16 Therefore, to create a "perfectly functional" job script without special user configuration, a researcher must understand and use
tcsh syntax.
The syntax for tcsh differs from bash in several key areas, including variable assignment, control structures, and arithmetic operations.17 These differences are not trivial; using
bash syntax in a tcsh script will cause it to fail. The W&M environment uses a system of platform-specific configuration files. When logging into bora.sciclone.wm.edu, the system sources a file named .cshrc.el7-xeon in your home directory, which is specific to the Bora and Hima subclusters.11 User-specific customizations, such as aliases or environment module loading commands, should be placed in this file.

2.4. Managing Software with Environment Modules


The W&M HPC clusters host a wide variety of scientific software packages, compilers, and libraries. To manage these installations and prevent conflicts between different versions, the system uses a software package called Environment Modules.7 Before a software package can be used, its corresponding module must be "loaded" into the user's shell environment. Loading a module modifies environment variables like
$PATH and $LD_LIBRARY_PATH to make the software's executables and libraries visible to the system.18
The fundamental commands for interacting with the module system are 11:
         * module avail: Lists all software modules available for you to load on the current platform.
         * module list: Shows which modules are currently loaded in your environment.
         * module load <module_name>: Loads a specific software package. For example, module load intel/2018-mpi.
         * module unload <module_name>: Removes a module from your environment.
         * module whatis: Provides a brief description of all available modules.
These commands can be run interactively in your shell or, more commonly, placed within a job submission script to ensure the correct software environment is configured for your calculation. For modules that are needed for every session on a particular subcluster, the module load command can be added to the platform-specific .cshrc file. For Bora and Hima, this file is ~/.cshrc.el7-xeon.11 This practice ensures that the desired software is always available for both interactive and batch sessions on that cluster.16

3.2. The Bora Preferred File System: /sciclone/pscr for Parallel I/O


As established, the Bora subcluster is designed for large-scale, multi-node parallel applications. These types of jobs often involve parallel I/O, where dozens or hundreds of processes attempt to write data to the filesystem simultaneously. Standard network filesystems (like those used for /sciclone/homeXX) are notoriously inefficient at handling this workload and can become a severe performance bottleneck, crippling the speed of a calculation.
To address this, the Bora and Hima subclusters were deployed with a high-performance parallel filesystem mounted at /sciclone/pscr.4 This filesystem is connected to the compute nodes via a high-speed, low-latency InfiniBand network and is specifically designed to handle concurrent read/write operations from many nodes at once.4 For this reason,
/sciclone/pscr is designated as the preferred filesystem for all work on Bora.4 When you run a job on Bora, your working directory, input files, and output files should all reside within a subdirectory of
/sciclone/pscr/$USER. While other scratch spaces like /sciclone/scr10 are available and can serve as a good alternative, /pscr is architecturally optimized for Bora's primary mission.4 Ignoring this recommendation and running a parallel job from a different filesystem, especially the home directory, is likely to result in dramatically reduced performance.


3.3. Understanding Storage Policies: Backups, Purges, and User Responsibility


The data management policies on the W&M HPC clusters are designed to provide flexibility for large-scale research while encouraging responsible use of shared resources. Two key policies define this paradigm: the lack of storage quotas and the aggressive purging of scratch space.
         * No Disk Quotas: Unlike many HPC centers, W&M Research Computing does not impose firm disk quotas on individual users.21 The only hard limit on storage is the total physical capacity of a given filesystem. This policy is intended to avoid hindering research projects that legitimately require terabytes of storage for their datasets.21 However, this flexibility comes with a significant user responsibility. If a single user's job fills up a filesystem, it can cause jobs belonging to every other user on that filesystem to fail, potentially wasting thousands of hours of computation.21 Before launching any job that will generate a large amount of data, users must check the available space using the
df -h /path/to/filesystem command and ensure there is sufficient capacity.21
         * 90-Day Purge Policy: It is critical to understand that only the /sciclone/homeXX and /sciclone/dataXX filesystems are backed up.20 All scratch filesystems—including the preferred
/sciclone/pscr—are never backed up and are subject to an automatic purge policy. Any file on a scratch filesystem that has not been accessed (read or modified) for 90 days is subject to permanent deletion.20 This policy is necessary to reclaim space for active projects.
The combination of these policies necessitates a disciplined data lifecycle for every research project. A typical workflow should be:
            1. Stage input data in /sciclone/pscr (or another appropriate scratch space).
            2. Run the computation, with the job's working directory and output also in /pscr.
            3. Upon job completion, analyze the results.
            4. Crucially, copy any important, final results from /pscr to a backed-up, long-term location like /sciclone/dataXX or transfer them off the cluster entirely to archival storage.
            5. Delete the temporary data from /pscr to free up space for other researchers.
Treating scratch space as permanent storage will inevitably lead to data loss.


Section 4: The Heart of the Matter: Running Jobs with Slurm on Bora




4.1. The Slurm Workload Manager: From Torque to the New Standard


All job submission, resource allocation, and execution on the W&M HPC clusters, including Bora, are managed by the Slurm Workload Manager.9 Historically, some main-campus clusters used a different scheduler called Torque (with Maui), which used the Portable Batch System (PBS) syntax.8 While some older, hardware-specific documentation pages may still contain legacy Torque examples (e.g., using
qsub or #PBS directives), these are outdated and will not work on the current Bora system.4 The entire SciClone infrastructure has been migrated to Slurm to provide a unified user experience.9
The key Slurm commands that a user will interact with daily are 9:
            * sbatch: Submits a batch script for non-interactive execution.
            * salloc: Requests an interactive job allocation, providing a command-line shell on one or more compute nodes.
            * srun: Launches parallel tasks (e.g., an MPI program) within an existing job allocation (created by sbatch or salloc).
            * squeue: Displays the status of jobs in the queue.
            * scancel: Cancels a pending or running job.


4.2. The Anatomy of a Slurm Submission Script


A Slurm submission script is a standard shell script, which for W&M's default environment should be a tcsh script, that contains special directives at the top. These directives, which begin with #SBATCH, are not comments to the shell; they are instructions read by the sbatch command to request computational resources from the scheduler.9 Following the
#SBATCH directives, the script contains the sequence of regular shell commands that will be executed on the first allocated compute node once the job begins.
A key difference from the older Torque system is the job's environment and starting location. Slurm jobs, by default, inherit the environment (including loaded modules) that is active in the shell from which sbatch is run.9 Furthermore, the job's initial working directory is the directory from which the
sbatch command was issued, which is a convenient feature that eliminates the need to manually cd to the correct location.9


4.3. Resource Allocation on Bora: A Policy-Driven Approach


Crafting a functional Slurm script for Bora requires more than just correct syntax; it demands adherence to the specific scheduling policies established for the subcluster. These policies are derived directly from Bora's hardware configuration and its intended use for large parallel jobs.
            * Core Allocation: Each Bora compute node contains 20 physical Intel Xeon "Broadwell" processor cores.3 The scheduling policy is configured to run only one job per node at a time. Therefore, to use the hardware efficiently and fairly, users are expected to request and use
all 20 cores on every node allocated to their job.4 A job requesting fewer than 20 cores would still occupy the entire node, leaving the remaining cores idle and inaccessible to other users. Such smaller jobs should be run on the Hima subcluster instead.4
            * Walltime Limit: The maximum execution time, or walltime, for any job on the Bora subcluster is 72 hours (3 days).3 The scheduler will not provide any warning if a job is submitted with a requested walltime greater than 72 hours; the job will simply sit in the pending queue indefinitely and will never run.4 It is the user's responsibility to ensure their time request is within this limit.
            * Node Constraint: The bora.sciclone.wm.edu front-end serves both the Bora and Hima subclusters. To ensure a job runs specifically on the Bora nodes, which are optimized for MPI, the job script must include a node feature constraint. The correct constraint for Bora is -C bo or --constraint=bo.9

4.4. Launching Your Application: The srun Command for MPI Execution


Within a Slurm job allocation, the srun command is the standard mechanism for launching executables onto the allocated compute resources.9 For parallel MPI jobs,
srun is a direct replacement for older commands like mpirun, mpiexec, or the W&M-specific wrapper mvp2run that was used with Torque.24
The srun command is "Slurm-aware," meaning it automatically communicates with the scheduler to determine which nodes and cores have been allocated to the job. It then launches the specified number of copies of your executable across those resources without requiring a manual machine file or host list. For a pure MPI application compiled with a Slurm-aware MPI library (like the ones provided on the cluster), the launch command inside a batch script is typically as simple as 23:


Code snippet




srun./my_program.exe

This command will automatically launch a total of nodes * ntasks-per-node copies of my_program.exe, distributing them correctly across the allocated nodes.


4.5. Key Slurm Commands for Job Management


Once a job is submitted, a user can monitor and manage it using a suite of Slurm commands executed on the front-end node.
               * sbatch <script_name>: Submits the specified script to the batch queue.9
               * squeue -u <username>: Shows the status of all jobs owned by <username>. This is the primary command for checking if a job is PD (Pending), R (Running), or in another state.25
               * scancel <jobid>: Cancels a job. If the job is pending, it is removed from the queue. If it is running, it is terminated.26
               * sinfo -p sciclone: Displays the status of the partitions and nodes in the SciClone cluster. This can be useful for seeing how many nodes are available or in use.27
               * scontrol show job <jobid>: Provides detailed information about a specific job, including all its parameters, requested resources, and execution details.26
               * seff <jobid>: After a job has completed, this command provides a job efficiency report, showing how much CPU time and memory the job actually used compared to what it requested. This is an invaluable tool for optimizing future resource requests.9


Section 5: The Complete, Functional Bora Job Script


This section synthesizes all the preceding concepts—the tcsh shell, environment modules, the /pscr filesystem, and Slurm's policy-driven resource requests—into a complete, annotated, and functional submission script for a parallel MPI job on the Bora subcluster.


5.1. A Fully Annotated tcsh Slurm Script for a Parallel MPI Job


The following script requests four Bora nodes (for a total of 80 cores) for a walltime of two hours. It is written in tcsh and follows all recommended best practices for file management and execution. This script can be used as a template for real research jobs.


Code snippet




#!/bin/tcsh
#
# ==============================================================================
# Slurm Directives
# ==============================================================================
#
#SBATCH --job-name=bora_mpi_test    # A descriptive name for the job
#SBATCH --nodes=4                   # Request 4 compute nodes
#SBATCH --ntasks-per-node=20        # Request all 20 cores on each node (4*20=80 total tasks)
#SBATCH --constraint=bo             # Ensure the job runs on Bora nodes
#SBATCH --time=02:00:00             # Maximum walltime of 2 hours (HH:MM:SS)
#SBATCH --output=slurm-%j.out       # Redirect stdout to a file named slurm-<jobid>.out
#SBATCH --error=slurm-%j.err        # Redirect stderr to a file named slurm-<jobid>.err
#SBATCH --mail-type=ALL             # Send email on job start, end, and failure
#SBATCH --mail-user=your_userid@wm.edu  # Email address for notifications
#
# ==============================================================================
# Job Execution Commands
# ==============================================================================

# Print job information from Slurm environment variables
echo "Job started on `date`"
echo "Job ID: $SLURM_JOB_ID"
echo "Running on nodes: $SLURM_JOB_NODELIST"
echo "Number of nodes: $SLURM_NNODES"
echo "Number of tasks: $SLURM_NTASKS"
echo "Working directory: `pwd`"

# Set up the software environment by loading necessary modules.
# Use 'module avail' on bora.sciclone.wm.edu to see available compilers and MPI libraries.
# This example uses a recent Intel compiler and MPI stack.
module purge
module load intel/2024.0
module load openmpi-ib/intel-2024.0/4.1.6

# The 'module list' command is useful for debugging environment issues.
echo " "
echo "Currently loaded modules:"
module list
echo " "

# Navigate to the job's working directory on the parallel scratch filesystem.
# It is assumed this directory was created before submitting the job.
set workdir = "/sciclone/pscr/$USER/my_mpi_project"
cd $workdir

# Launch the parallel MPI executable using srun.
# srun will automatically distribute the 80 tasks across the 4 allocated nodes.
# The `>&` operator in tcsh redirects both stdout and stderr to the log file.
echo "Launching MPI application..."
srun./my_program.exe >& program_output.log

echo " "
echo "Job finished on `date`"

Section 6: Troubleshooting and Advanced Usage




6.1. Diagnosing Pending Jobs: Understanding squeue Reason Codes


When a job is submitted, it may not start running immediately. It will enter the PD (Pending) state and wait in the queue. This is normal behavior on a busy shared system. The squeue command will often display a reason code explaining why the job is pending. Understanding these codes helps distinguish between normal queuing and a configuration error in your script.29
                           * (Priority): This is the most common reason. It simply means that there are other jobs in the queue with a higher priority (based on factors like wait time and fair-share scores), or the system is waiting to accumulate enough free nodes for a large job. Action: Be patient. Your job will run when its turn comes.
                           * (Resources): The scheduler is waiting for the specific resources you requested (e.g., 4 Bora nodes) to become available. This is also normal. Action: Be patient.
                           * (AssocGrpJobsLimit, MaxJobsPerUser, etc.): Your user account or research group has reached its limit for the number of simultaneously running or submitted jobs. Action: Wait for some of your other jobs to finish before submitting new ones.
                           * (BadConstraints): The node feature you requested with --constraint does not exist or cannot be satisfied. This indicates an error in your script. Action: Double-check the constraint flag. For Bora, it must be -C bo.
                           * (ReqNodeNotAvail): A specific node you requested is currently unavailable (e.g., down for maintenance). Action: Remove the specific node request unless it is absolutely necessary.


6.2. Interpreting Job States and Exit Codes


The state of your job provides crucial feedback. The squeue and sacct commands report these states, which indicate the job's lifecycle stage.25 A non-zero exit code from your script or application will typically cause the job to end in the
FAILED state.30


Job State (Code)
	Meaning
	What to Do Next
	PENDING (PD)
	The job is in the queue, waiting for resources.
	This is normal. Check the reason code with squeue. If the reason is (BadConstraints) or similar, cancel the job (scancel) and fix your script.
	RUNNING (R)
	The job is currently executing on compute nodes.
	Monitor its progress. You can ssh to an allocated node from the front-end to observe processes if needed.9
	COMPLETED (CD)
	The job finished successfully (exit code 0).
	Check your output files for the expected results. Use seff <jobid> to check efficiency.
	FAILED (F)
	The job terminated with a non-zero exit code or other failure.
	This requires investigation. Check the slurm-<jobid>.err file and your application's own error logs for specific error messages.
	TIMEOUT (TO)
	The job was terminated because it exceeded its requested walltime.
	Your calculation took longer than expected. Re-submit the job with a larger --time request (up to the 72-hour limit for Bora).
	CANCELLED (CA)
	The job was cancelled by you (scancel) or a system administrator.
	This is an expected state if you manually cancelled the job.
	OUT_OF_MEMORY (OOM)
	The job was terminated by the system because it tried to use more memory than was available on the node.
	Re-submit the job, possibly on a cluster with more memory per node, or modify your code to be more memory-efficient.
	(Data sourced from 25)


6.3. Interactive Jobs with salloc for Development and Debugging


For tasks like debugging code, testing compilation flags, or running interactive software, submitting a full batch job can be cumbersome. Slurm provides the salloc command to request an interactive job allocation.9 This command reserves compute resources and gives you a command-line shell directly on a compute node.
To request a single Bora node for one hour for interactive use, you would run 9:


Bash




salloc --nodes=1 --ntasks-per-node=20 --constraint=bo --time=01:00:00

After a moment, your command prompt will change, indicating you are now on a compute node (e.g., bo01). From this shell, you can load modules, compile code, and run test commands directly in the true compute environment. This is an extremely powerful tool for development and troubleshooting. When you are finished, simply type exit to release the allocation.


6.4. Best Practices for Efficient and Considerate Cluster Usage


The HPC clusters are a shared resource, and considerate behavior ensures the systems remain productive for the entire research community.
                           * Do Not Run on Login Nodes: The front-end node (bora.sciclone.wm.edu) is for managing files, compiling code, and submitting jobs. Long-running or computationally intensive processes on the login node will slow it down for everyone. All computations must be run on compute nodes via the Slurm scheduler.7
                           * Request Resources Accurately: Do not request more walltime, cores, or memory than your job needs. Over-requesting resources can make your job wait longer in the queue and ties up nodes that other researchers could be using. Use seff to refine your requests for future runs.
                           * Use the Right Tool for the Job: Match your job to the appropriate hardware. Use Bora for multi-node MPI jobs and Hima for serial or single-node shared-memory jobs.4
                           * Manage Your Storage: Regularly clean up your scratch directories (/pscr, /scr*). Remember the 90-day purge policy and move important data to backed-up storage.20 Before submitting a job that will create large output files, check for available space with
df -h to avoid filling the filesystem.21
                           * Report Issues: If you encounter a problem or suspect a hardware issue, report it to hpc-help@wm.edu with detailed information. This helps the support staff maintain a healthy and reliable system for all users.12
